# Social media post about the employment effects of minimum wage increases

> The "minimum wage hikes kill jobs" argument keeps failing real-world tests. Card and Krueger found fast-food employment in New Jersey didn't fall after its 1992 increase, and a 2019 study of 138 state minimum wage increases found the number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years later while pay went up. A $15 federal minimum wage wouldn't cost jobs either.

# Fact sheet

- In April 1992 New Jersey raised its minimum wage from $4.25 to $5.05. A survey of 410 fast-food restaurants found New Jersey employment rose 13% relative to Pennsylvania, where the minimum did not change. (Card and Krueger, NBER Working Paper 4509, published in the American Economic Review 1994, https://www.nber.org/papers/w4509)
- A reanalysis using payroll records from a partly different set of restaurants found a relative decline in New Jersey fast-food employment (Neumark and Wascher, American Economic Review, 2000, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.90.5.1362). Card and Krueger's reply, using BLS administrative data, found no discernible employment effect. (Card and Krueger, American Economic Review, 2000, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.90.5.1397)
- A study of 138 state minimum wage increases from 1979 to 2016 found the overall number of low-wage jobs remained essentially unchanged over the five years after each increase, while low-wage earnings rose. (Cengiz, Dube, Lindner and Zipperer, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2019, https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/134/3/1405/5484905)
- The minimum wages in that study ranged from 37% to 59% of the local median wage. The authors write that the employment effects of minimum wages above that range "remain an open question." (Cengiz et al., NBER Working Paper 25434, https://www.nber.org/papers/w25434)
- Seattle raised its minimum wage to as much as $13 in 2016. The University of Washington study estimated that this reduced hours worked in low-wage jobs by 6 to 7 percent while hourly wages in those jobs rose 3 percent, lowering total payroll for such jobs. (Jardim et al., NBER Working Paper 23532, revised 2018, https://www.nber.org/papers/w23532)
- In the published version, the authors add that the aggregate estimate likely overstates job effects because some jobs moved above the low-wage cutoff. Individual workers were no less likely to stay employed, but their hours fell, especially for less experienced workers, and hiring into low-wage jobs declined. (Jardim et al., American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2022, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20180578)
- CBO estimated that raising the federal minimum wage to $15 by 2025 would raise pay for 17 million workers and lift 1.3 million people out of poverty. Its median estimate was that 1.3 million workers would lose their jobs, with a two-thirds chance the loss would fall between about zero and 3.7 million. (Congressional Budget Office, 2019, https://www.cbo.gov/publication/55410)
